	\begin{abstract}
		\noindent RISC-V is a global technology whose impact
                varies by region. In Europe, various communities
                gathering both academics and industrials interested in
                RISC-V and open-source hardware organised local events
                early on. After a while, it became clear that a
                continent-wide event was needed. This article
                summarizes how organizers of some of these events got
                together and joined forces with RISC-V International
                to create the “RISC-V Summit Europe” series, one of
                the three world-class RISC-V Summits.
	\end{abstract}
}

\section{Introduction}

The emergence of the open ISA RISC-V created numerous opportunities in
circuit design and software: (1) opening up the field of \textmu
architecture to scientific research; (2) providing opportunities for
states and industries to make independent choices for computing cores;
(3) enabling the emergence of new collaboration models between
research and industry; and (4) the possibility for software to impact
hardware solutions.

Before this, the duopoly of Intel/AMD and ARM on processor cores made
research in \textmu architecture very difficult. Their proprietary
nature prevented the implementation of the instructions sets. Even
when access to the source code of commercial cores was granted, the
licensing issues made it difficult to collaborate about and experiment
with new hardware features or extensions. Therefore, besides in
Cambridge (UK), the knowledge of ISA implementation and processor
design was very scarce in Europe, as other teams working on CPU design
were based mostly in the USA, in Israel, and a couple of other
locations.

So the potential benefits of RISC-V for Europe came with several
challenges: (a) there were very few curriculum for processor design at
the master or PhD level; (b) both academia and industry had very small
teams knowledgeable in processor design; (c) there was neither
national nor European ecosystems that could take on RISC-V; and (d),
due to the proprietary nature of existing ISAs, the software community
was taking existing architecture as a given, and was not used to
collaborate with hardware communities on processor design.  In other
words, everything had to be constructed almost from scratch.

This paper tries to summarizes a key point of the construction of the
European RISC-V ecosystem: how the burgeoning European RISC-V
community went from several regional, small-scale events to the
\emph{RISC-V Summit Europe}, one of the three world-class event of the
global RISC-V community

The remaining of this document is organized as follows: First a
presentation of the various RISC-V events that emerged organically
locally; Second, details on the gathering of a several groups involved
into these local events towards a larger, European event to unify our
ecosystem; Third an overview of the first four \emph{RISC-V Summit
Europe}; And lastly a conclusion with some perspectives.

\subsection{France}

\input{table-fr}

The French events --~see Table\;\ref{table-fr}~--
started in October 2018, and expanded gradually in scope and openness
until May 2022. They have been basically co-managed by CEA and INRIA

A benefits of RISC-V is to enable actual collaboration on hardware and
software research, i.e. co-design.  Although things have changes since
then, in 2018 the national ecosystem was such that INRIA was one of
the main institutions dedicated to software research, with some
activities in \textmu architecture, while conversely, the CEA carried
out a large part of chip design activities, with some work in basic
software.

After attending the workshop in Barcelona in May 2018, the
\citetitle{MTG-1} \cite{MTG-1} was set up on short notice, on October
2018 in Grenoble, and was organised in a hurry by a committee of CEA
researchers.  Presenters and attendees were academics, and attendance
was on invitation only.  The \citetitle{WEEK-1} \cite{WEEK-1}, in
October 2019 in Paris, was a dual event. The main event was expanded
to 2 days. Its organisation committee welcome individuals from other
research institutions, like \href{https://inria.fr}{INRIA},
\href{https://www.univ-ubs.fr/fr/index.html}{UBS}, or
\href{https://tima.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr}{INPG/TIMA}, together with
some industrials like
\href{https://www.thalesgroup.com/en/research-and-innovation}{Thales
  R\&T} and
\href{https://www.st.com/content/st_com/en.html}{STMicroelectronics}. The
presentations were from both academia and industry and still on
invitation. One of the new development was the association with a
research community, \citetitle{GDR-SOC2} \cite{GDR-SOC2} to add a
one-day \emph{side event}. Registration to the 2+1 days were open to
everyone for the first time. The \citetitle{WEEK-2} in March/April
2021, was the first step towards international cooperation. First by
welcoming European researchers into the organization committee, and
second by inviting \citetitle{OPENHW}, a then Canada-based foundation,
to organize a one day side event. Participation was open, but
presentations at the 2+1 days event were still on invitation. Then the
\citetitle{WEEK-3} \cite{WEEK-3}, in May 2022, was the first really
international event we had in France, with a proper \gls{pc},
\gls{cfp}, and six exhibitors. One of the wise decisions made by the
\gls{pc} was to accept as posters the submissions that were not
accepted for the plenary while still being relevant to the community.

\section{Towards a European event}

The road to the 1st European-wide event, the \citetitle{BCN-2023} was
a two-steps process. During the summer of 2022, organizers of the
local events mentioned above joined together into an informal
\gls{sc}. Then during the Fall of 2022, the \gls{sc} joined forces
with \citetitle{RISCV} to put together a shared organisation to run
the upcoming event.

\subsection{European Organizers, Unite!}

During the \citetitle{WEEK-3}, held in Paris on early May 2022,
discussions began on the possibility of organizing a Europe-wide
event. An informal agreement was quickly reached to host the next
event at BSC in Barcelona, tentatively scheduled for late spring 2023.

The discussions kept going throughout the 2022 summer on how to get
together. Keep in mind that even though organizers of local events
were very excited about RISC-V and a Europe-wide event, we had a
couple of issues to address: (1) Although there were previous
collaborations within the group, not everybody knew everybody else;
(2) What kind of balance should be maintained among the different
countries? (3) What kind of balance should we have between academia
and industry?

We ended up quickly putting together a \gls{sc} of 13 people: 4 from
Spain, 4 from Austria and Germany, 4 from France, and 1 from
Switzerland. For the groups with 4 seats, it was agreed that they
would pick 2 people from industry, and 2 from academia. This went
smoothly and we quickly started the \gls{sc} work.

The first \gls{sc} decisions were to define a prefered event format,
how to build its program, define the team that would be in charge, and
decide where the events would be held. We easily reached a strong
consensus on each of these issues.

We went for a yealy event, on site only -- no online broadcast. Our
format would be a full week of 1+3+1 days. With 1 day for \glspl{twg}
and \glspl{sig} meetings, and tutorials. Then 3 days for the main
conference. And finally 1 day for \emph{side events}, mostly workshops
(open or closed) of publicly funded collaborative projects. The main
program would include sponsors keynotes, selected presentaions, and
invited talks and keynotes. A \gls{cfp} would be issued, and
submissions will be reviewed by a \gls{pc}. The best submissions would
be presented in plenary sessions while most of others would be
accepted as posters.  For the location, we agreed that the three first
events would be hosted by the three main countries: Barcelona in 2023,
Munich in 2024 and Paris in 2025. As for the team, we picked a
\gls{lcc} close to the \gls{sc}\footnote{The first three \gls{lcc},
for Barcelona, Munich and Paris were actually all academic \gls{sc}
members.}, figured we should hire a local \gls{pco}, and, of course,
we wanted team up with \citetitle{RISCV}!

\subsection{Joining forces with RISC-V International}

The discussion with \citetitle{RISCV} went smoothly, and a fruitfull
three parties collabration was quickly agreed on: The \gls{lc} would
be made of the \gls{sc} (which appoints the \gls{lcc}),
representatives from \citetitle{RISCV}, and a local \gls{pco}. The
Summit would be recorded and the videos posted online, together with
the slides, extended abstacts and posters.

\citetitle{RISCV} would take in charge the search for sponsors, the
contractualization with the \gls{pco}, the organisation of the
\glspl{twg} and \glspl{sig} meetings and tutorials on Monday, and the
marketing of the event at large -- including, no less, the
\emph{RISC-V Summit Europe} branding to match in Europe the two others
word-class Summits in North-America and China. \citetitle{RISCV} was
also instrumental in atracting key speakers for keynote and talks.

The \gls{sc} would appoints the \gls{lcc} and the \gls{pcc}. The
\gls{lcc} is responsible to secure a venue, propose tentatives date
for the summit, put together an off-site social event, gather
volunteers to run the event, and indentify potential \glspl{pco}. The
\gls{pcc} would take in charge the review process to rank
submissions. The \gls{sc} would also take care of calling for and
organising the Friday side events.

The \gls{pco} would be in charge of the registration web site, while
the \gls{sc} would manage the summit web site. For the latter, the
goal is to have a simple web site that could be kept online for ages.

\section{The RISC-V Summits Europe}

The joint team worked smoothly: decisions were
consensus-based, inluding the \gls{cfp}, selection of invited keynotes
and talks, exhibition and poster stands layout, and final
adjustements to the main program. The first four Summits were very
successfull -- see Table\;\ref{table-summits} -- with attendees from
Europe, and a sizeable attendance from Asia, North America, and the
Middle East.

\input{table-summits}

Over time we extended the Summits Europe with addditional features. To
name a few: \emph{Hackathons} on Monday; A \gls{cfp} for
\emph{academic demos} to run after the sponsors demos of Tuesday and
Wednesday (in 2025 \& 2026); \emph{Special sessions} of
90\;min.\ outsourced to external communities (e.g. about RISC-V in
Space, and on the EU Project Tristan in 2026); And \emph{VIP events}
to energise the locals RISC-V ecosystems (in 2024, 2025, and 2026) --
even though they are not, strictly speaking, part of the Summit.

On
the internal organisation, we aim to pass on unwritten knowledge
by having the \gls{pcc} and \gls{lcc} of the years $N-1$ and $N+1$ as
co-chairs for year $N$, working with the current chair.

\section{Conclusion and future editions}

Each of the three wolrd-class Summits has its local flavor and
characteristics. The RISC-V Summit Europe seems to be vey much about
research and innovation, with lots of presentations from PhD students
and researchers. Another key aspect of the European RISC-V and open
hardware ecosystem, is that academics and industrials involved in
these technologies do know each other, and most of them are attending
the Summit Europe. This is thanks to the numerous EU-funded R\&D
initiatives where both public researchers and commercial companies
work together on collaborative projects. This is very promising for
the future of the European ecosystem. The \gls{sc} itself reflect
this, as it includes both academics and industrials from several
countries.

After going through the three locations agreed on during the original
merge, Bologna and its vibrant ecosystem were selected for the 2026
edition. For the future events, we are working on a formal process to
select the location. There are several conflicting issues to address:
(1) the chosen location should have a vibrant RISC-V ecosystem and
local industry; (2) to ensure success, it is important that the
\gls{lcc} and the \gls{pcc} are somehow close to the \gls{sc}; (3) in
Europe, a rotation between countries and major cities of the continent
is required.
It is crucial that we communicate these European specificities to a global, non-European audience.
